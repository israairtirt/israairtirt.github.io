Defending a travel agency's branch network

A travel agency branch had two incidents on record: a rogue wireless access point listening to staff traffic, and a DNS server taken down by a flood of UDP packets. I reviewed the network, planned its addressing, linked it to the London head office and designed defences for both attacks.

Role
Network security analyst, sole author
Scope
LAN review, addressing, VPN, physical security, incident analysis, policy and host controls
Guidance
NIST SP 800-61, 800-41, 800-77, 800-94, 800-153; ISO/IEC 27001:2022; CISA; NCSC
Year
2026

Scenario project. The agency is fictional and both incidents come from the case scenarios in NIST SP 800-61. The defences follow current guidance from NIST, CISA, the NCSC and the ICO.

The network as I found it

A tree: two access switches feed a core switch, which sits behind a firewall, a border router and a cable modem. Two DMZ switches hang off their own firewall legs. The finding that mattered most was the first-floor wireless access point. It was plugged straight into the router, outside the firewall, so anything that joined the Wi-Fi bypassed every control on the network. The core switch and the single firewall were the other weak points: if either failed, the whole branch went down.

Addressing

I split one private /24 with VLSM into four /27s for the user segments and three /28s for servers and devices, which leaves 80 addresses free for growth. A small public /29 covers the router, the firewall and the static NAT entries for the public servers. Infrastructure gets fixed addresses; staff devices use DHCP.

Linking to London, and the server room

The branch reaches head office through a site-to-site IPsec VPN on the firewall: IKE for the key exchange and ESP with AES for the traffic. Running it on the firewall saves a device, but it adds load and makes the firewall an even bigger single point of failure. The servers on the first floor get a locked room and the physical controls from ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A.7, such as controlled entry and CCTV.

Incident 1: the rogue access point

Classified as an external, unstructured access attack: someone without special skill or resources plugged in a device and listened. The defences, in the order I'd fund them:

  1. Move the real access point behind the firewall on its own segment.
  2. WPA3-Enterprise with 802.1X. EAP-TLS is preferred over PEAP because it needs a certificate, not just a password. WPA3 also makes protected management frames mandatory.
  3. Wireless intrusion detection and prevention to find rogue devices, plus the quarterly rogue-AP scans that PCI DSS 11.2.1 already requires of anyone taking card payments.
  4. Staff awareness training (ISO/IEC 27001 A.6.3), so unknown devices get reported.

Incident 2: the DNS flood

Classified as an external, structured denial-of-service (DoS) attack: a sustained flood of UDP packets from one address, always from the same source port. DNS is attractive to attackers because a small query can return a large answer; CISA puts the amplification factor at 28 to 54.

Around those sits the NCSC's four-step approach: understand the service, understand the defences, write a response plan, and test it.

Policy and the manager's workstation

I reviewed two existing security policies against the rogue access point incident and rewrote the clauses that would not have stopped it. For the manager's workstation, the host controls start with full-disk encryption using BitLocker and a TPM, with the recovery keys backed up away from the laptop. If personal data is exposed and a risk to people is likely, the ICO must be told within 72 hours, so that duty is written into the incident plan.
