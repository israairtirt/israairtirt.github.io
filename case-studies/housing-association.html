Securing a housing association's tenant data

A small UK housing association was about to take rent online. Its servers were unpatched, staff shared one cloud login, the website ran over plain HTTP and tenant details travelled on WhatsApp. I assessed the risks and designed the fixes.

Role
Security analyst, sole author
Scope
Risk assessment, vulnerability triage, network redesign, data protection
Frameworks
ISO/IEC 27001:2022, NIST SP 800-94, UK GDPR, PCI DSS v4.0.1
Year
2026

Scenario project. The association is fictional; the vulnerabilities, standards and law are real and current.

What needed protecting

I started from the assets, not the technology: tenants' personal data, payment records (which fall under PCI DSS once rent is paid online), signed leases, maintenance logs and staff communications. Maintenance logs matter more than they look. Under Awaab's Law a landlord has 24 hours to act on an emergency hazard, so losing them is a legal problem as well as an operational one. Communications carry a second, less controlled copy of everything else.

Risk register

Fifteen asset and threat pairs, each rated for likelihood and impact, with a written reason for every rating. One came out Very High: exploitation of the unpatched public servers. Eight were High, five Medium and one Low. Each threat then got a primary control, an alternative and a justification.

Four critical vulnerabilities

The server inventory listed exact versions, so I traced each one to its CVEs and checked them against NVD and CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue.

The network, redesigned

  • A next-generation firewall with IPS and a default-deny rule base, and a screened subnet for anything the public can reach. The database moves out of the DMZ into the internal network. HTTPS has to stay open, so the web server sits behind a WAF and is patched first.
  • An always-on, full-tunnel TLS VPN on port 443, authenticated with certificates and MFA. It answers the auditors' concern about staff on public Wi-Fi, and it works on the mix of devices staff actually use. IPsec would have suited company laptops only.
  • Network IDS in the DMZ and the internal network, host IDS on the servers and wireless IDS in the office, each placed for the attacks the others miss.
  • AES-256 for data at rest, RSA-2048 signatures over SHA-256 for leases, and Triple DES ruled out, since NIST stopped allowing it for encryption after 2023.
  • TLS 1.2/1.3 with HSTS, the correct and look-alike domains registered, SPF, DKIM and DMARC on email, and one account per person with MFA in place of the shared cloud login.

WhatsApp and UK GDPR

Staff were sending tenant details on WhatsApp from their own phones. That fails Articles 5(1)(f) and 32: a message can go to the wrong person, a lost phone exposes every chat, and the association can't monitor, keep or delete what was sent, so it can't show it complies. The ICO reprimanded NHS Lanarkshire for the same practice in 2023. I recommended that senior management approve a policy backed by a data protection impact assessment, fund a managed messaging tool, make training mandatory and follow the rules themselves.

What I took from it

  • Check the exact version. Log4j 2.15 looks patched and isn't, which is how I learned to confirm that a fix actually took.
  • Draw the trust zones before the boxes. The diagram only made sense once I'd decided what each zone was allowed to reach.
  • Write the reason next to every rating. Doing that made me lower one of my own scores when the reasoning didn't hold up.
